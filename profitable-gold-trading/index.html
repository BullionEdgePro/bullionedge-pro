Trading gold profitably

It is arithmetic with two inputs, and most traders never deliberately choose either of them. Here is the maths, including the part that says nobody can promise you a profit.

The honest answer to "is gold trading profitable" is that it is profitable for a minority of the people who attempt it, and that the ones who get there rarely do it by being better at predicting price. They do it by fixing two numbers that most traders never deliberately choose at all.

Those two numbers are how much you lose when you are wrong and how much you make when you are right. Everything else — the entry technique, the indicator, the news read — is a rounding error next to them. This page shows the arithmetic, because it is arithmetic, and because once you have seen it you cannot unsee what a 1:1 target does to an account.

The number almost nobody calculates

Every reward-to-risk ratio has a win rate at which you break even. Below it you lose money however good your entries are; above it you make money however ordinary they are.

Reward : riskWin rate needed to break evenWhat that means
1 : 1 50% You must be right more than half the time just to stand still — before spread and commission.
1 : 1.5 40% Being wrong 60% of the time is survivable.
1 : 2 33% Being wrong 67% of the time still makes money.
1 : 2.5 29% Being wrong 71% of the time still makes money.
1 : 3 25% Being wrong 75% of the time still makes money.

Read the bottom row again. At 1:3 you can lose three trades out of every four and still be ahead. That is not a trick and it is not optimism; it is division. The reason most retail accounts die at 1:1 is not that those traders were bad at charts — it is that they set themselves a bar (50% accuracy, forever, after costs) that professionals do not clear either.

The three ways accounts actually die

Nobody loses an account by being slightly wrong about direction a lot of times. They lose it in three specific ways, and each one has a mechanical answer.

1. Oversizing

One position four times larger than the others, taken because it felt certain. Eight good decisions erased by one exception. The answer is not discipline — it is not being asked: position size should be an output, never an input. Give the software your stop and your risk percentage and the lot size falls out of them. In BullionEdge Pro lots round down, so the real risk lands under the budget rather than over it, and there is no field anywhere that accepts a lot size you chose yourself.

2. Revenge trading

The trade after the loss, taken to get it back, at twice the size and a quarter of the thought. It is the most expensive habit in retail trading and it is completely predictable: it happens after losses, when tired, and when down for the day. So the desk shuts on a daily loss limit, and asks about state before it will produce a plan. Those questions are the one part of this software that still asks you anything, because they are the one thing candle data cannot answer.

3. Trading when there is no edge

Thin sessions, the weekend gap, the ninety seconds either side of a Fed decision. The same setup that works at the London–New York overlap is a coin flip at 3am, and around a release your stop is not the number you calculated because price can gap straight through it. The desk holds a calendar of the releases that move gold and refuses outright inside those windows.

What this software cannot do

It cannot tell you where price is going. There is no forecast anywhere in it, its score is a measure of how well a setup follows a rulebook rather than a probability of profit, and its own win-probability estimate is capped at 82% because no pre-trade analysis justifies a higher claim.

It cannot promise you a profit, and neither can anybody else. Trading gold on leverage carries a real risk of losing more than you deposit. Most people who try it lose money. A tool that removes the three failure modes above has given you the conditions in which an edge can show up and compound — it has not given you the edge, and any product that tells you otherwise is selling you something that does not exist.
